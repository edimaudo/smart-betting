1. Betting overview

What betting is

A bet is a contract: you stake money on a specific outcome, and a sportsbook pays you a pre-agreed price if that outcome happens. Everything downstream — odds, payouts, edge — follows from that simple contract.

How sportsbooks and markets work

A sportsbook sets a market for an event — for example, a moneyline market for who wins outright, a spread market for margin of victory, or a total market for combined points. Each market has two or more selections, each priced with its own odds. Odds move as money comes in and as the book's own view of the event changes; comparing odds across books and across time is a core input to analysis.

How odds and payouts work

Odds express both a price and, implicitly, a probability. Decimal odds of 2.20 mean a $10 stake returns $22 total ($12 profit) if you win. American odds of +150 mean a $100 stake wins $150 profit; American odds of −150 mean you must stake $150 to win $100 profit. If the outcome doesn't happen, the stake is lost.

Probability, implied probability, and margin

Every price implies a probability — the break-even chance of winning at that price. Sum the implied probabilities of every selection in a market and the total is normally above 100%. That excess is the bookmaker margin (the "vig" or "juice"): the sportsbook's built-in edge, independent of who actually wins. Removing it (de-vigging) is required before comparing a model's probability to the market's fairly.

2. Key terms

Searchable glossary

No terms match "".

Moneyline
A bet on which side wins the event outright, with no margin of victory involved.
Spread
A handicap applied to the margin of victory so both sides are priced closer to even odds.
Total
A bet on the combined score of both teams relative to a line set by the sportsbook.
Over / Under
The two sides of a total bet: whether the combined score finishes above (over) or below (under) the line.
Prop
A "proposition" bet on a specific occurrence within an event, separate from the final result — e.g. a player's point total.
Parlay
A single bet combining multiple selections; all of them must win for the parlay to pay out, which multiplies both the payout and the risk.
Decimal odds
Odds expressed as a single multiplier of the stake, including the stake itself — e.g. 2.50 returns 2.5x the stake.
American odds
Odds expressed relative to a $100 stake — a positive number is profit on a $100 bet, a negative number is the stake needed to profit $100.
Implied probability
The break-even win probability represented by a given price, before removing the bookmaker's margin.
Vig / juice
The bookmaker's built-in margin, embedded in the odds rather than charged as a separate fee.
Bankroll
The total pool of money set aside for betting, used as the base for sizing individual stakes.
Unit
A standardized stake size (e.g. 1% of bankroll) used so bet sizes can be compared independent of bankroll size.
Edge
The gap between a model's estimated probability and the market's (de-vigged) implied probability for the same outcome.
Expected value (EV)
The average profit or loss a bet would produce if the same price and probability were repeated many times.
Closing line
The final price available before an event starts, widely treated as the market's most efficient estimate of an outcome's true probability.
Line movement
How a price changes between opening and closing, reflecting new information or the balance of money on each side.
Push
A result that exactly matches the line (e.g. a spread bet landing on the exact margin), returning the stake with no win or loss.

3. Betting approaches

Fundamental analysis

Evaluating the underlying factors that drive outcomes — rosters, injuries, matchups, weather, travel.

Statistical analysis

Using historical rates and distributions rather than narrative to estimate the likelihood of outcomes.

Market-based analysis

Treating the market price itself, and how it moves, as the primary source of information.

Trend-based analysis

Looking at recent form or streaks — useful as a signal, but prone to overweighting small samples.

Value-based analysis

Asking whether a price is favorable relative to true probability, rather than only asking who will win.

Model-driven analysis

Producing a probability estimate from an explicit, repeatable model rather than judgment alone.

Risk-based approaches

Sizing and selecting bets according to bankroll management and variance tolerance, not just expected return.

4. Upsides & downsides

⚠ Betting involves financial risk. Structured analysis can improve decision quality over time, but it does not guarantee any individual outcome. Only wager money you can afford to lose.

Analytical benefits

A structured process — probability, edge, EV, risk — replaces guesswork with an explainable, repeatable framework.

Financial risk

Every bet risks the full stake. Positive expected value over time does not prevent losses on any single bet.

Variance

Short-run results can diverge substantially from long-run expectation, even when the underlying edge is real.

Model error

Every model rests on assumptions that can be wrong, incomplete, or stale relative to the current matchup.

Data limitations

Historical and market data can be sparse, delayed, or unavailable for some markets, weakening any conclusion drawn from it.

Behavioral risks

Chasing losses, ignoring a strategy's own rules, and stopping analysis after a win are common, costly patterns.

Overconfidence

A model producing a precise-looking number does not make that number more certain than the data underneath it.

5. Strategic thinking

Every opportunity in this application is evaluated through the same chain of reasoning:

Probability→ Odds→ Implied Probability→ Edge→ Expected Value→ Risk

A price only becomes interesting when your estimated probability is meaningfully higher than what the market's price implies (edge), that edge translates into positive expected value, and the risk involved fits your strategy. The goal is never to simply predict who will win — it's to evaluate whether the available price justifies taking the risk.
