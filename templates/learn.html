What a bet actually is

A bet is a contract. You stake money on a specific outcome, and a sportsbook pays you a pre-agreed price if that outcome happens. If it doesn't, you lose the stake. Every other concept on this page (odds, probability, edge, expected value) is just a way of reasoning about that one contract more precisely.

How markets and prices work

A sportsbook sets a market for an event: a moneyline market for who wins outright, a spread market for margin of victory, or a total market for combined points. Each market has two or more selections, each with its own odds. Prices move as money comes in and as the book's own view of the event changes, which is why comparing odds across books and across time is a useful input to analysis.

Probability, implied probability, and margin

Every price implies a probability: the break-even chance of winning at that price. Add up the implied probabilities of every selection in a market and the total is normally above 100%. That excess is the bookmaker margin, often called the "vig" or "juice": the sportsbook's built-in edge, independent of who actually wins. Removing it ("de-vigging") is required before comparing a model's probability to the market's fairly.

How people try to estimate that probability

Nobody has a direct line to the true probability of an outcome, so different approaches try to estimate it in different ways. Most real analysis blends several of these:

Fundamental

Rosters, injuries, matchups, weather, travel: the underlying factors that plausibly drive the outcome.

Statistical

Historical rates and distributions rather than narrative, used to estimate likelihood directly.

Market-based

Treating the price itself, and how it moves, as the primary source of information.

Trend-based

Recent form or streaks. A useful signal, but easy to overweight given how small the sample usually is.

Value-based

Asking whether a price is favorable relative to true probability, not just who is more likely to win.

Model-driven

An explicit, repeatable model produces the probability estimate, rather than judgment alone.

Risk-based

Sizing and selecting bets by bankroll management and variance tolerance, not expected return alone.

What that means for risk

⚠ Betting involves financial risk. A structured process can improve decision quality over time, but it guarantees nothing for any individual bet. Only wager money you can afford to lose.

A structured process (probability, edge, expected value, risk) replaces guesswork with something explainable and repeatable, but it doesn't remove the underlying risk. Every bet risks the full stake, and a real edge can still lose in the short run: variance means results over a handful of bets can diverge a lot from the long-run expectation. Models rest on assumptions that can be wrong or stale, and data can be sparse or delayed, especially for less common markets. The most common and most avoidable failure isn't a bad model; it's behavior: chasing losses, ignoring your own strategy's rules, or treating a single win as proof a decision was correct.

The strategic frame this app uses

Every opportunity in this application is run through the same chain of reasoning:

Probability→ Odds→ Implied Probability→ Edge→ Expected Value→ Risk

A price only becomes interesting when your estimated probability is meaningfully higher than what the market's price implies (edge), that edge translates into positive expected value, and the risk fits your strategy. The goal is never to simply predict who will win. It's to evaluate whether the available price justifies taking the risk.

No terms match "".

Moneyline
A bet on which side wins the event outright, with no margin of victory involved.
Spread
A handicap applied to the margin of victory so both sides are priced closer to even odds.
Total
A bet on the combined score of both teams relative to a line set by the sportsbook.
Over / Under
The two sides of a total bet: whether the combined score finishes above (over) or below (under) the line.
Prop
A "proposition" bet on a specific occurrence within an event, separate from the final result, such as a player's point total.
Parlay
A single bet combining multiple selections; all of them must win for the parlay to pay out, which multiplies both the payout and the risk.
Decimal odds
Odds expressed as a single multiplier of the stake, including the stake itself. For example, 2.50 returns 2.5x the stake.
American odds
Odds expressed relative to a $100 stake. A positive number is profit on a $100 bet; a negative number is the stake needed to profit $100.
Implied probability
The break-even win probability represented by a given price, before removing the bookmaker's margin.
Vig / juice
The bookmaker's built-in margin, embedded in the odds rather than charged as a separate fee.
Bankroll
The total pool of money set aside for betting, used as the base for sizing individual stakes.
Unit
A standardized stake size, such as 1% of bankroll, used so bet sizes can be compared independent of bankroll size.
Edge
The gap between a model's estimated probability and the market's de-vigged implied probability for the same outcome.
Expected value (EV)
The average profit or loss a bet would produce if the same price and probability were repeated many times.
Closing line
The final price available before an event starts, widely treated as the market's most efficient estimate of an outcome's true probability.
Line movement
How a price changes between opening and closing, reflecting new information or the balance of money on each side.
Push
A result that exactly matches the line, such as a spread bet landing on the exact margin, returning the stake with no win or loss.
